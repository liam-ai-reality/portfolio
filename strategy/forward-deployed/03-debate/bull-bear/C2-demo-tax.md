# C2 — The Demo Tax: bull and bear

## Bull

**It solves the cold-start problem, which nothing else here does.** Constraint C-4
says there is no audience and nothing will be carried by reputation for six months.
Every other candidate has to manufacture demand from scratch. This one borrows it:
the artefact being torn down has already proven that thousands of people care, and
the title can say so. For a channel starting at zero this is worth more than any
other single property.

**It is NDA-immune by construction, not by discipline.** Constraint C-2 is the thing
most likely to quietly strangle this channel. Every other candidate manages that risk;
this one eliminates it. There is no version of this format where Liam accidentally
says too much, because there is no client in the room.

**It spends renewable receipts.** The security toolkit (R4) does real work on screen
every episode and never depletes. Compare C1, which burns finite career narrative
three episodes in and then has nothing left.

**The format is the sales pitch.** A buyer watching Liam find nine production defects
in something they were about to greenlight has just watched a demonstration of the
service. No call to action is required; the video *is* the argument.

**It is genuinely under-served.** The market read found the "gap between a demo and
a system" almost entirely unfilmed, despite being universally needed. Everyone
building AI has this problem and nobody is showing them it.

## Bear

**The audience skews to people who cannot buy.** This is the real objection. The
median viewer of a teardown of a viral agent tutorial is an individual contributor
developer, and IC developers do not sign consulting contracts. D1 is weighted 25 for
a reason, and this candidate scores mid at best. It generates reach that may not
convert.

**Tonal risk is severe and hard to detect early.** The format is one bad take away
from being a dunking channel. Dunking performs extremely well on engagement metrics
and extremely badly on inbound — nobody hires someone they watched be unkind, and
the metrics will look *better* as the business outcome gets worse. That is the most
dangerous possible failure mode: a wrong signal that feels like a right one.

**It positions Liam as a critic rather than a builder.** Sustained over a season, the
audience's mental model becomes "the person who finds problems in other people's
work". That is a lower-status and less commercially useful identity than "the person
who ships the thing", and it is hard to escape once established.

**Production cost is at the ceiling.** 7–9 hours against an 8-hour budget, and
teardowns have a habit of overrunning because the interesting failures are found
mid-build. The fixed checklist mitigates this but does not remove it.

**Dependency risk.** The format needs a steady supply of popular public artefacts
worth tearing down. That supply is currently abundant, but it is not under Liam's
control, and a season could stall waiting for a suitable target.

**Possible reputational cost in a small market.** The AI engineering world is not
large. Tearing down the wrong person's work could close doors — including with the
labs, which is directly relevant to the secondary goal.

## Where the bear is answered

Tone risk is real but manageable by construction rather than by care: credit the
original as a good demo that never claimed to be a system, and make the second half
of every episode a fix rather than a failure count. That converts "critic" into
"builder" within the same episode.

The audience-composition objection is **not** answered, and should not be. It is
correct. C2 is a reach engine, not a conversion engine, and treating it as the whole
strategy would be the mistake. See ADR-002.
